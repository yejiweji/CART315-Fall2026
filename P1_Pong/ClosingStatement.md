# Closing Statement: Mother Pong

## 1. Reflection on the Entire Process
Going into this project, I honestly wasn’t sure where I wanted to take it. I just knew I didn’t want it to be generic or predictable; I wanted a genuine intention behind whatever I built. Because I didn't have the luxury of time on my side, I couldn't rely on heavy visuals to carry the game like I used to do. I had to let the raw mechanics do the heavy lifting. In a previous reflection, I talked about how I tend to be very explicit in guiding my players, and how I feel like that over-direction is exactly what makes my games feel lackluster in my own eyes. I had to fight that urge here. 

My starting point for changing Pong was simply to pull from a place of personal interest and stop worrying about calculating the exact next steps for what the player is "supposed" to do. I don't actually have a lot of experience playing games myself, I usually prefer watching people play and listening to their commentary. I realized that when I watch others, I don't really focus on the granular mechanics; I focus on the overall experience the game is able to deliver (for the player and even those in the room), like whether it gives people something meaningful to comment on. I wanted to create something the player could be involved in, but where they didn't necessarily need to be in active control to feel the weight of the experience.

## 2. Evaluation of Outcomes
Lately, I have been learning about family dynamics and relationships in another class, and it has been on my mind a lot, especially enmeshed dynamics. People in those situations tend to completely miss the total lack of control they have because it is wrapped up in a false semblance of safety. Shifting the paddle from the player's hands to "Mother's" hands successfully materialized that exact feeling of creeping, invisible erosion.

But as the prototype came together, the outcome expanded into a broader critique of systemic trust that surprised me. It made me think about how in-game help bots or power-ups are always packaged as if they are for our benefit, when the game could easily be using them to keep you losing just so you spend more time playing or feel incentivized to buy advantages (The game that comes to my mind is COOP app). We are so quick to trust a game, just like we are so quick to trust our families and our loved ones. Maybe those who love games the most are the ones who are the most trusting of games and their bots. Watching the final bot-vs-bot stalemate loop forced me to look at the artifact and ask; *What gives us such a blind sense of trust that a system or a person is looking out for us, when that can so easily not be the case?* 

## 3. Reflections Carried Forward
This project completely changed how I think about design and structural control moving forward:
* **Designing for the Bystander:** Since I value the experience of watching and commenting on games over playing them, I want to carry forward the idea of designing games that remain powerful even when control is stripped away. The narrative shouldn't stop when the player stops pushing buttons.

* **Critiquing Exploitative Design:** I want to take this exploration of enmeshment and apply it directly to modern software and UI design. The trust we give to maternal figures is the exact same trust tech companies exploit through predatory algorithms, invasive assistant bots, and manipulative feedback loops. Moving forward, I want to use minimal, visual-light mechanics to expose those hidden traps.
