I kept thinking about Schön’s idea of a designer developing an internal “gyroscope.” He describes it as the ability to look at your own work and recognize when something is good or not, even if you cannot completely explain why. I like that he does not treat this as something a designer either has or does not have. It develops through years of making things, reflecting on them, and learning to recognize quality for yourself.

As someone who is still pretty new to prototyping, I find this reassuring because I often have trouble knowing whether a mechanic is actually good or if it is just working. While making Mother Pong, I kept making decisions and then questioning whether they were actually improving the game. For example, I made Mother’s paddle glow red when she takes control. It clearly communicates that something has changed, but I started wondering if the effect was too obvious. Maybe leaving the paddle unchanged would make the loss of control more subtle and make the player realize what is happening later. I do not really have enough experience yet to know which choice makes the game “better.” Schön’s discussion made me realize that this uncertainty is probably part of learning how to design.

The Growltiger example also made me question how much a designer can or should control the meaning of their work. In class, we have put a lot of emphasis on intention, especially through our MDM journal process, but the example showed how players can find uses or meanings that the designer never expected. If a player finds meaning in something I made that I never intended, does that make the design less successful, or does that meaning become part of the game? Maybe developing a “gyroscope” is not about eventually knowing the one correct answer to whether a game is good. It is about becoming better at noticing what is and is not working, while accepting that my judgement can change as I gain experience and see how other people experience my work.
