# Design Journal: Elevator Simulator

## Journal Update #1:

Near the end of my first prototype, I thought about adding details to the stairwell, like railings, floor numbers, and fire exit signs. I left them out because I wasn’t sure they would add anything. I figured less information would make the stairwell more unsettling, but after submitting it, I felt like I had taken that too far. The environment felt pretty empty, and I’m not sure it gave off the feeling I wanted.

Not long after, a fire alarm went off in my condo. I had only moved in about a week before, so I was still getting used to the building. I had been told we would get an email if an alarm was just a test, and I hadn’t received one, so I immediately questioned whether it was real. I live on a higher floor, so I couldn’t wait around to find out. I started heading downstairs in case something was actually happening. What made it stressful was not knowing what other people knew. I didn’t know if anyone else was taking it seriously, if they had gotten some kind of notice, or if they assumed it was another test. I was also a little embarrassed about possibly overreacting, but I didn’t want to be careless either. On the way down, I was hoping to run into someone who knew what was going on, like a staff member who could tell me if it was a drill. Instead I ran into a mother with a baby, and I felt like I had to step up even though I was just as confused as she was. There weren’t many people around, and the few I did see didn’t have answers. Hearing their worries sometimes made me more panicked. This made me want to add other people to my game. In the first version, the player was completely alone. I still want to keep the feeling of not knowing what’s going on, but I think other people could add something. The player might expect someone to know what’s happening, or look to them for reassurance, and find out they’re just as confused.

I plan to add details you’d normally find in an apartment building, like railings, floor numbers, and fire exit signs, and give the player an actual starting point instead of dropping them straight into the stairwell. Second, I want to add a few characters the player can approach. They could ask what’s happening, but the answers wouldn’t really help. Some characters might not know anything, and others might share their own worries. I want to see if this makes the game feel more like a situation someone could actually end up in. I don’t want to lose the original idea of the endless stairwell, but I think there’s more I can do with it than making it empty and repetitive.


## Journal Update #2

I started adding some of the details I was missing from the first version. I added handrails, doors, and exit signs above the doors. I also took some photos of the stairwell in my condo to get a better idea of what makes an apartment stairwell feel realistic. I noticed that a lot of it comes down to how ordinary everything looks. The doors, lighting, signs, and walls aren't particularly interesting on their own, but together they make the space feel familiar. I wanted to bring some of that into my game so the player has a better sense of where they are.

I also started thinking about whether I should add a fire alarm sound effect. It would make sense given what inspired this version, but I'm worried it would turn the game into more of a fire drill simulator. I still want to focus on creating a feeling of panic rather than recreating the actual experience of evacuating a building. I did consider making the doors interactive, so the player could try opening them or looking through them. During the actual fire alarm, I remember looking out into the hallways and noticing how oddly quiet they were. I think being able to check the doors and see what's happening outside could add to the experience, especially if the player doesn't know what to expect. For now, I've just added simple doors as placeholders.

One thing I wanted to change from the first version was the lighting. I mentioned before that the stairwell was starting to look a bit like Roblox, and I think part of the reason was how empty and repetitive everything felt. I decided to make the lighting more consistent throughout the stairwell instead of relying on shadows and dramatic lights. I also kept it relatively dim. I wanted the lighting to feel more like what you'd actually find in an apartment building. There's something about the same sterile lighting, concrete walls, and staircases repeating over and over again that I find uncomfortable. Nothing really changes, so the player doesn't get much of a chance to prepare themselves for what's coming. I think keeping the environment ordinary makes it harder to tell when something is wrong.

I also started thinking about how I would act if I were actually in this situation. During the fire alarm, I remember feeling like I had to maintain a certain level of composure in case I ran into someone. I didn't know who I would encounter or whether they would be more worried than I was. I think I want to bring some of that into the game. The player shouldn't just be worried about getting downstairs. They should also be unsure of who they might run into along the way.


## Journal Update #3: 

I added a handyman to the stairwell as a stand-in for the other people I eventually want to include. I want the player to encounter different kinds of people while making their way downstairs, including staff members who seem like they would know what's happening and more vulnerable people, like children or someone with a baby. I think both types of encounters could make the situation more uncomfortable in different ways.

I originally wanted to include someone like a staff member because, during my own experience, I remember hoping I would run into someone who could tell me what was going on. Someone who seemed like they knew the building and would have more information than I did. But what if even that person doesn't know what's happening? I think encountering someone who you would normally expect to rely on, only to realize they're just as confused, could make the player feel even more helpless. On the other hand, encountering someone more vulnerable could make the player feel like they need to take responsibility and help them, even though they don't know what's going on themselves. That's something I experienced when I ran into the mother and her baby during the fire alarm. I wasn't just thinking about getting myself downstairs anymore. I felt like I had to be someone they could rely on, even though I didn't feel particularly confident myself.

For now, I've only added the handyman as a placeholder. I think having a mix of people who don't know what's happening, people who seem like they should know, and people who might need help would add more to the experience than having the player walk down an empty stairwell. It also means that encountering someone doesn't automatically make the player feel safer. Sometimes it might just give them another reason to worry.

Moving forward, I would like to create a hallway for the player to explore and look into from the stairwell doors as well as possibly a room to begin in rather than spawning inside the stairwell of the get go.
