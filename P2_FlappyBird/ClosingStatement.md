# Closing Statement: Flappiest Bird

Looking back, Flappiest Bird made me reconsider what it means to make a successful game. I started with the idea of pushing Flappy Bird's addictive elements as far as I could, but I ended up questioning whether there is even a single answer to what makes a game engaging. This brought me back to Pippin Barr's The Stuff That Games Are Made Of and the idea that the "stuff" of a game isn't limited to what the developer intends. Players bring their own experiences, interests, and ways of playing to it. They can create their own game within the game, finding meaning or enjoyment in things the developer never anticipated.

I think this also applies to addiction. The mechanics I added might keep one player engaged while pushing another away. My prototype reflects what I think would hold my own attention, but that doesn't make it the best version of the game for everyone. Personally, I think more animated elements and a smaller colour palette would hold my attention more, but adding background music or sound effects might actually make me fatigue faster. It made me realize that more stimulation doesn't necessarily mean a better experience, and that the type of stimulation matters too.

Maybe the goal shouldn't always be to make the best game, as if there were one universal standard to meet, but to make something meaningful to the people who play it. A game can be successful because players find comfort in it, relate to it, or enjoy it in their own way, even if that isn't what the developer originally intended.

The ethical side of this is harder for me to answer. Deliberately designing around reward and emotional triggers raises questions about whether we're creating something people genuinely enjoy or capitalizing on responses that keep them coming back. I don't think I can draw a clear line between the two, especially when the same mechanic can be helpful or harmful depending on the person and how it's used. I'm left questioning what success actually means for a game. Is it how long someone plays, how much they enjoy it, or what they take away from the experience? When I started this project, I thought I was trying to figure out what makes a game addictive. Now, I'm not even sure that has one answer. What keeps me playing might push someone else away, and what makes a game meaningful to one person might mean very little to another. Maybe making a successful game isn't about finding a formula that works for everyone, but understanding that there isn't one.
