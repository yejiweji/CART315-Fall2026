# Motivation Statement: TBD Flappy Bird

Flappy Bird was such a popular and addictive game that its developer, Dong Nguyen, eventually removed it from the App Store despite its success. It honestly blows my mind that a game so simple could have such a grip on people that its creator felt guilty about the effect it was having on them. I wanted to understand what made it so difficult to put down, and through researching its success, I found that some of its mechanics resemble the reward systems used in slot machines. The instant restart, near misses, frustration, and constant possibility of beating your high score all encourage you to try again. It made me question how much of a game's success comes from the game itself and how much comes from the way it triggers our brains to keep wanting more.

When approaching a game to make, I usually ask myself, "What is the best game I can make?" However, Flappy Bird made me question what makes a game the best in the first place. I couldn't imagine how I could improve on a game that has already received such a successful response, but I started wondering what would happen if I stripped away the very elements that made it so addictive. Would the game still be fun on its own, or would removing those mechanics completely ruin its appeal and charm? Could players find their own enjoyment in it, or would there be nothing left to keep them coming back?

For this prototype, I want to experiment with removing or changing these addictive elements and see what remains. I am interested in whether Flappy Bird can still stand on its own without the constant cycle of frustration, anticipation, and reward. It makes me question how much of what we consider good game design is actually about creating a meaningful or enjoyable experience, and how much is about finding ways to keep players hooked.

I also keep thinking about the developer's response to the game's success. He could have seen how strongly people reacted to it and treated it as proof that he had found the formula for a successful game. Instead, he removed it because of his concerns about its effects on players. This raises a question for me about the responsibility developers have when they discover how easily their games can keep people playing. If these psychologically triggering mechanics are what make a game successful, should developers be trying to replicate them, or should they be questioning the impact they have? Through this prototype, I want to explore what makes Flappy Bird engaging by deliberately taking those elements away, and see what that reveals about the relationship between game design, enjoyment, and addiction.
