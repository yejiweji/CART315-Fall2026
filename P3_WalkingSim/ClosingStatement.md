# Closing Statement: Walking Simulator

I think this project was almost the opposite of what I was trying to achieve with my previous prototype, Flappiest Bird. With Flappiest Bird, I was experimenting with how much I could do to keep a player engaged and hold their attention. I was constantly thinking about what could make someone want to keep playing, whether that was through rewards, animations, or other forms of stimulation. With this project, I found myself doing the opposite. I wanted to strip away as much as possible and see whether the lack of information could become the experience itself. It's interesting how two projects that both explore player behaviour ended up taking such different approaches.

Throughout the process, I considered adding more details to the stairwell, like signs, floor numbers, or drawings on the walls. However, I felt these would give the player too much information and make the environment feel too intentionally unsettling. I wanted players to question their surroundings rather than be given an obvious story to follow. This also influenced my decision not to include things like a health bar or level counter. In real life, we don't have visible indicators telling us how long we've been doing something or how much further we have to go. We have to keep track of those things ourselves, whether that's estimating how much time has passed or counting each flight of stairs. I think removing these familiar game mechanics makes the experience feel more grounded in reality, which can make it even more unsettling.

If I were to continue developing this project, I'd experiment with more subtle environmental details while building on the audio. For example, the number or arrangement of railing sections could follow a pattern connected to the game's lore, or the concrete textures could hint at what year the building is from. I like the idea of players gradually noticing things that don't quite make sense without necessarily understanding what they mean. I'd also like to experiment more with footsteps, distant noises, and echoes that make the player question whether someone else is nearby. I actually found exploring audio really fun, and I think combining it with subtle visual details could make the experience more immersive. Rather than giving players more information, I'd want to give them just enough to make them question what they're seeing and hearing.

I also think this project could benefit from being experienced in VR. Since so much of the experience depends on the player feeling physically present in the stairwell, I imagine having to look around, turn corners, and listen to sounds coming from different directions would make the environment much more immersive. Even though horror games usually establish an expectation that the player is going to be scared, I think there's something different about placing someone in an ordinary-looking environment where they don't know what to expect. Without an obvious threat or indication that something scary is about to happen, the player might become more aware of their own surroundings and start questioning things that would otherwise seem completely ordinary.

Ultimately, this project made me realize that creating engagement doesn't always mean giving the player more to look at or interact with. Sometimes, withholding information can be just as effective as adding it. I started with the intention of recreating the panic of having to make a decision without knowing whether it's the right one, but I ended up exploring how much of that feeling could come from simply taking away the things that normally give us a sense of control. I think what makes this experience interesting is that the player isn't necessarily being forced into anything. They can stop, turn around, or choose another direction at any point. However, they still don't know whether any of those decisions will help them. Maybe that's what makes the experience unsettling. It's not necessarily about being afraid of what's around the next corner, but about the uncertainty of not knowing whether the choices you're making are actually getting you anywhere.
